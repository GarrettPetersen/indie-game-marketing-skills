# Playtesting, festivals, press, and creator outreach

## Playtesting

Treat playtesting as product development, not promotion. Recruit players who
could plausibly enjoy the genre, while also testing with people unfamiliar with
the project. Observe where they hesitate, misunderstand, quit, repeat a session,
or voluntarily return. Separate crashes and defects from comprehension,
balance, taste, and feature requests.

Maintain a reproducible report path with build identifier, platform, input,
steps, expected result, observed result, logs, and save when relevant. Look for
patterns across testers without flattening strong minority signals.

When the game supports the browser, a low-friction web build can broaden device
coverage and simplify access. Use explicit opt-in diagnostics to collect
structured bug and performance incidents, and pin immutable builds when a
festival or reviewer needs a durable link. See
[web-playtest-telemetry.md](web-playtest-telemetry.md).

Do not recruit creators under a promotional pretext and then expect unpaid QA.
If early creator access is knowingly rough, say so using exact human-written
language and make reporting optional.

## Festivals and showcases

Use directories for discovery, then verify every candidate on its official
site. Track:

- audience and genre fit;
- event and deadline dates with timezones;
- eligibility and release-state restrictions;
- cost, travel, exclusivity, discount, and build obligations;
- required media, keys, hardware, and localization;
- Steam event visibility versus off-platform exposure;
- date applied, confirmation, selection date, deliverables, and result.

Do not distort tags or public positioning to fit a weak festival. Favor events
whose actual audience, platform visibility, feedback, or relationships justify
the application work.

## Creator and press research

Research people who demonstrably cover comparable games, not broad gaming
lists alone. Keep a source-backed contact table with public contact route,
language, platform, audience size, recent activity, comparable title, fit
reason, priority, verification date, outreach status, and follow-up.

The developer supplies the exact outreach email, subject line, and follow-up.
The agent may insert only verified factual fields into that human-approved
template. Do not invent praise or claim someone played a game without evidence.
Sending the same concise human-written message to many well-matched creators is
better than generating artificial one-off prose.

Start with a small batch to validate deliverability, links, positioning, and
response handling. Scale only when the process works. A stable full build can
give creators more to show; a public demo removes key friction. Choose based on
the campaign goal and the build's readiness, not a universal rule.

Treat keys as credentials. Verify an unsolicited requester through a public
contact route controlled by the claimed creator or outlet; do not trust the
incoming display name alone. Prefer Curator Connect when the intended recipient
is available there. Issue keys in small, labelled batches and record the
recipient, package, issue date, and status. Use Steam Playtest rather than
press keys for ordinary external testing, and never publish unused keys in a
public document or repository.

Press releases, pitches, biographies, application answers, and social posts
must also come from human-provided exact text. The agent can assemble press-kit
files, validate facts and links, and identify missing answers.

No form submission, message, key distribution, or publication occurs without
explicit authorization immediately before the external action.
