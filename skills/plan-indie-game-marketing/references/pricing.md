# Pricing and discount strategy

Choose price early enough that scope, positioning, regional pricing, launch
discounts, and revenue scenarios agree. Price the promise a prospective buyer
can see before purchase, then use experience with the finished game to check
whether that promise is fulfilled.

## Price perceived value before purchase

The governing question is not "How much content did the developer make?" It is
"What does a target customer who has not played yet expect this game to be
worth?" That expectation is formed from the capsule, trailer, screenshots,
tags, demo, reviews, trusted coverage, developer reputation, and the scope and
genre promise communicated on the store page.

Adding content that prospective buyers do not know exists does not by itself
increase launch willingness to pay. It may still improve demo response,
reviews, refunds, playtime, recommendations, retention, and long-term sales.
It raises the supportable price only when players can see credible evidence of
the added value before buying, or when post-launch reputation makes that value
visible to later buyers.

Do not compensate for weak positioning by counting features internally. Fix
the visible promise or choose a price that the existing promise supports. Do
not exaggerate, spoil discoveries merely to justify a tier, or list a large
quantity of low-value features.

## Start with useful default anchors

For a small commercial Steam game, use `$7.99 USD` as the initial hypothesis.
For a substantial indie game, use `$14.99 USD` as the initial hypothesis. These
are practitioner defaults, not platform requirements or universal optimums.

Judge size by the value a prospective player can reasonably infer: visible
depth, promised playtime and replayability, demonstrated variety and polish,
multiplayer or social value, external proof, and the scope and quality of close
competitors. Do not price from team size, development time, personal sacrifice,
file size, a private feature inventory, or the word "indie."

Move away from the defaults only with evidence. A very small, immediately
shareable game may work below `$7.99`; a deep strategy, simulation, survival,
or established-studio game may support `$19.99` or more. A short game can still
command a higher price when its audience and close comparables demonstrate that
value. Do not undercut competitors automatically.

## Understand the visibility feedback

Do not claim that Steam has disclosed a pure revenue-only algorithm. Valve says
that purchases and play are strong interest signals, and that prominent store
surfaces reflect what players spend time and money on. It also says store-page
traffic and conversion rate alone do not determine visibility.

The practical lesson remains: a lower price is not automatically safer. It must
create enough additional purchases and play to offset the revenue surrendered
per copy, and it may change the perceived value of the game. Higher gross
revenue can also qualify a game for revenue-ranked or popularity-driven store
surfaces. Model the feedback between price, paid units, play, word of mouth,
reviews, refunds, revenue, and resulting visibility rather than maximizing unit
conversion in isolation.

Ordinary price sensitivity still exists; platform feedback does not abolish a
demand curve. It makes the optimal launch price more complex than "cheaper
means more buyers" or "higher price means more revenue."

## Research the market price

Build a comparison set from recent games sharing the primary genre, audience,
visible promise, presentation quality, and business model. Record:

- undiscounted base price at launch and today;
- launch discount and later discount history;
- what a shopper can see or credibly infer about depth, playtime,
  replayability, variety, and multiplayer before purchase;
- review volume, sentiment, and recurring value complaints;
- developer reputation, existing intellectual property, and Early Access use;
- regional-price differences when material; and
- estimated units or revenue only when the method and uncertainty are stated.

Do not anchor on a blockbuster, an abandoned failure, a sale price, an old game
whose catalog discounting has matured, or games that merely share broad tags.
Read what positive reviewers believe justified the price and what negative
reviewers felt was missing.

## Test the store promise with representative non-players

Show the current store package to representative target customers who have not
played the game and do not reveal the proposed price. Ask what price they expect
and why, then record the distribution rather than only the median. The package
must match what an ordinary shopper will actually see; do not brief participants
with an invisible feature list.

Treat stated willingness to pay as evidence, not a guaranteed purchase. Give
more weight to players resembling the intended customer and compare their
answers with actual prices and reception of close games. If tester expectations
strongly disagree with the proposed tier, diagnose whether the issue is price,
visible scope, presentation, audience selection, or an unclear genre promise.

Separately ask experienced playtesters whether the delivered game feels worth
that price. This after-play test measures fulfillment, refund and review risk,
and word-of-mouth potential. It must not be substituted for the non-player test
of initial purchase expectations.

## Plan regional prices and discounts

Use Steam's current suggested regional prices as a baseline, then adjust only
where current purchasing-power, comparable-price, currency, tax, or market
evidence supports it. Verify that every supported currency has a valid price;
a missing or sub-minimum regional price can make the game unavailable there.

Plan base price and promotions together. Model the actual launch price after
the launch discount, later wishlist-notification thresholds, regional prices,
refunds, taxes, platform share, and a gradual discount ladder. Do not inflate
the base price solely to display a large launch discount, and do not rush to a
deep discount after launch in a way that punishes early buyers or exhausts later
marketing beats.

Steam changes discount limits, cooldowns, duration, notification thresholds,
and regional tools. Retrieve the current official documentation immediately
before entering or changing prices. Entering or submitting pricing is an
external action and requires explicit authorization.

## Output

Produce an internal pricing decision record containing:

- recommended USD base price and confidence;
- the closest pricing comparables and why they qualify;
- non-player expectations from the store package and after-play fulfillment
  evidence, kept separate;
- regional-pricing method and exceptions;
- launch discount and first-year discount ladder;
- gross and net revenue scenarios at several paid-unit outcomes;
- assumptions, risks, and the condition that would change the price; and
- the date on which platform rules and market evidence were checked.
